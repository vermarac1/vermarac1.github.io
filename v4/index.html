Shyam Spectra · NaaS and Enterprise Connectivity

From consumer broadband to enterprise B2B

The challenge

Spectra was transitioning from a consumer broadband business to an enterprise connectivity company. The buyers were different, the sales cycle was different and the proposition was different. The existing consumer brand equity did not automatically transfer.

Marketing had no enterprise positioning, no ICP, no demand engine and no shared qualification process with sales.

What we put in market

Three flagship products. Retail in a Box, bundled plans sized to different store formats. AWS AI VIS, camera software sold alongside the network so a retailer could see footfall and hot zones, and a warehouse could see where loading was going wrong and where stock was walking. And Wi-Fi Pro, a low-cost network designed for students living in paying guest accommodation. I also ran warehousing and PG end to end as verticals.

And I sold it myself

On Wi-Fi Pro I did not just market it. We printed the pamphlets, ran the email campaigns, and I went out and met PG operators in Bangalore and sold it to them. Once you have done that, sales stops seeing you as the person who sends leads.

What I did

  • Rebuilt the enterprise positioning and narrative
  • Defined the ICP from conversion data rather than assumption
  • Trained the team on the technology before launching any campaign
  • Realigned agencies and vendors around the enterprise proposition
  • Built a multi-channel GTM motion across outbound, content, paid, PR and events
  • Rebuilt marketing-to-sales qualification and handover
  • Cleaned the CRM, matched every lead to ICP and persona, then ran jobs-to-be-done per segment before writing a single sequence
  • Validated every sequence with the line heads, product managers and key account managers who actually sat with the customer
  • Automated it in HubSpot across a 7,000-lead pipeline
  • Moved the design language towards the enterprise register, because a buyer decides whether you are a serious enterprise company in about three seconds of looking at you
  • Established a fortnightly operating cadence across ten stakeholder groups
60%Engagement 25%Email engagement 7,000+Leads managed 35%Brand visibility 15%Monthly follower growth
The lesson

A repositioning is a sequencing problem before it is a creative problem.

The business had to keep selling while its identity changed underneath it. The work was not simply about changing the message. It was about changing the organisation's ability to understand, market and sell the new proposition.

Worth separating the two levers rather than presenting them as one achievement. Pipeline volume came from the multi-channel demand motion. Lead quality came from the qualification process we built with sales rather than handed to them. Their number moved because of something they helped design.

NayaOne · FinTech SaaS

Refilling a dry enterprise funnel with £3,000

The problem

The funnel had gone quiet. The obvious answer would have been to spend more. I did not think that was the problem.

The target market was small, high-value and risk-sensitive. I diagnosed it as an access and credibility problem rather than a budget problem.

The recommendation

I recommended content syndication as the channel, and going direct to TechTarget rather than routing the spend through an agency. That decision is what made the economics work. It removed the agency margin, let me negotiate the terms myself, and meant the deal could be structured around demonstrated intent instead of impressions.

What I did

  • Recommended content syndication over more paid spend, after diagnosing the constraint as access rather than budget
  • Went direct to TechTarget instead of through an agency, and ran the negotiation myself
  • Proposed the £3,000 programme to the CEO and got it signed off
  • Researched and wrote the white paper myself rather than briefing it out
  • Structured the commercial terms around demonstrated intent, not impressions
  • Routed the returned names straight to inside sales with the context attached

The lesson

In a small enterprise market you do not need more reach. You need better access to the people already signalling intent.

Shyam Spectra · Spectra One customer platform

A platform stalled for two years, delivered in six

The challenge

Spectra One was a single portal where an enterprise customer logged in and saw every location, every network service, the plans they paid for, the devices configured per location, billing and invoicing, speed limits and rates, network status, downloadable reports, and could raise a complaint.

It had been attempted twice and had gone nowhere for nearly two years. The reason was not features. Business Excellence, CRM, Customer Experience, Product and IT each owned a piece of the data and nobody owned the whole thing.

The resistance was disbelief, not disagreement. People treated it as the third attempt that would also quietly die. I had no authority over anyone in that room.

What I did

  • Stopped calling it a marketing project and ran it as a business programme with a board commitment behind it, which changed whose problem it was
  • Set a weekly cadence and held it even in the weeks people did not turn up, because what I could do without authority was make the cost of not delivering visible in a room every week
  • Put everything in writing. PRDs, feature documents and customer journey maps, so scope stopped being a conversation and became a document
  • Sat with the CRM and customer teams to clean and standardise data nobody had ever reconciled
  • Defined the API requirements with IT so the data could actually flow
  • Led UI and UX direction with external agencies
  • Pulled the COO in deliberately and sparingly, because he could move people I could not
  • Did the unglamorous detail work myself first, because after two failed attempts nobody was going to believe this one until they saw someone doing it

The lesson

Two years of discussion had produced nothing because there was nothing anyone could disagree with specifically. A document changes that.

AgreeYa Solutions · Funnel definition and attribution

Making a lead mean the same thing to marketing and sales

The challenge

Marketing and sales had no shared definition of what a lead was at any stage of the funnel. Every handover was ambiguous, and more importantly there was no way to connect anything marketing did back to the revenue number.

The approach

Define the whole funnel with sales rather than defining it and handing it over. Lead, MQL, SQL, Opportunity, Win, with explicit criteria for what moved something from one stage to the next.

Then work backwards from the actual revenue target. Wins needed, based on average ticket size. Opportunities to produce those wins. Then SQLs and MQLs, using historical performance, benchmarks and expected conversion at each stage. Rebuild the plans around what the funnel required rather than around a list of activity.

What made it stick

  • Signed off by sales and by the CEO. A definition agreed in a meeting gets relitigated the first time someone does not like a lead. A definition signed off by the CEO does not
  • Tied marketing's target to revenue rather than lead volume, deliberately, because that puts marketing in front of risk it was not carrying before
  • Tracked conversion at every stage, MQL to SQL to opportunity to win, and tied it back to the revenue target
  • Built the measurement layer early rather than at the end, because the question of whether marketing was worth the money was always coming

Marketing had been a support function until then, so proposing the definitions meant walking into sales' territory. The accountability is what bought the right to be there.

5Funnel stages defined and signed off 1,000+Qualified leads 25%Lead conversion improvement 10,000+Unique visitors in year one, from zero
The lesson

Sales will only accept your definitions if you accept being measured on the outcome. It has to cut both ways, or it is just marketing telling sales what a good lead is.

The change I would point to is not a number. It is that marketing and sales stopped arguing about whether a lead was good or bad based on opinion, because there was a definition and there were numbers to work against.

Systems Lifecycle · Small software company

The job where marketing and sales were one job

Where it started

There was no pipeline and no lead generation process. Outreach was untargeted email with no digital acquisition behind it at all. I fixed the website, then brought in Google Ads as the first scalable channel. The very first campaign produced a lead, and that lead became a meeting, which sounds small and was the proof the business needed.

There was no wall between marketing and sales to get across, because I was not operating as a marketing person. I set up the campaign, generated the lead, spoke to the prospect, qualified them, arranged the meeting, sat in the demo and helped build and send the proposal.

What actually moved the numbers

We changed what we measured. We stopped reporting that marketing had generated a number of leads and started asking whether those leads became meetings, then demos, then proposals, then customers.

Then the market changed

A wave of low-cost competitors appeared with products addressing broadly the same need. As a small company we were not going to win a price fight, so there was no point pretending otherwise.

  • Treated it as a distribution problem rather than a pricing problem, because if you meet a cheaper competitor in the same channel the buyer compares you side by side on price and you lose that comparison every time
  • Our buyers already worked closely with lawyers, compliance professionals and company secretaries, who held the trust we were trying to build cold
  • Proposed a partnership-led acquisition model and went looking for the right professional services firms rather than for volume
  • Built the first relationship with a legal firm where the exchange genuinely ran both ways. They introduced our software to clients who needed it, we introduced their services to customers who needed those
  • Tested the model before we committed to it as a direction, and was explicit that it added a route rather than replacing the existing effort

The lesson

In a small company marketing and sales should not be two functions at all.

When you are personally in it from the first click through to the proposal, you see exactly where people drop off and why, and that feedback improves the marketing in a way no report ever does. The second lesson came later. When competitive pressure increases you do not always need to compete harder. Sometimes you change the route to the customer, because reaching someone through a person they already trust beats shouting louder in a market that has got crowded.

The method

How I get investment for something nobody has tried yet.

Four steps. Used six times, at six companies, over eleven years.

01

Bring a model, not a case.

Projections for what the channel should return at each stage. Audience, engagement, cost per click, cost per lead, conversion. It stops the conversation being "do we believe in marketing", which is a question of faith and one I will always lose, and makes it "do we believe these assumptions", which is arguable.

02

Ask for a controlled start, not a budget.

A pilot, with what we expect to see written down in advance. Asking for full budget on a channel nobody has tested is how you get a no, and deservedly.

03

Measure it properly or the small start is meaningless.

Click-through, cost per click, cost per thousand and cost per acquisition, tracked from day one. If you cannot measure the pilot, starting small is just spending less money badly.

04

Let the evidence buy the next round.

Scepticism is not settled by one good presentation. I keep putting the evidence in front of people as it comes in, rather than making the case once and getting on with executing.

Used

Where it has been used.

LinkedIn as a second channel at Systems Lifecycle. The whole digital function at AgreeYa, website and SEO before paid. A $250,000 demand-generation budget at AgreeYa. Content syndication proved on one pilot with aMarketForce before it became a programme. £3,000 direct to TechTarget at NayaOne. The paid budget at BlueKaktus.

Also

And the other half of it.

When a channel stops working I change the route rather than pushing harder. Email to content syndication at AgreeYa. A dry funnel to bought intent at NayaOne. A price war to a partnership channel at Systems Lifecycle.

The operating model in one line

Customer insight, positioning, channels, execution, funnel measurement, optimisation, revenue. Scale changes how you execute it. The customer still has a problem, and the job is still to understand that problem better than anyone else and build a measurable path from it to revenue.

Point of view

How I think about marketing.

Six positions I arrived at by getting some of them wrong first.

01

Start with the business problem.

I do not start with channels. I start with the commercial objective, the buyer, the constraint and the number the business needs to move.

02

Position before promotion.

If the market does not understand the proposition, more media usually creates more noise rather than more demand.

03

Build the funnel as a system.

Acquisition, qualification, nurture, conversion and handover are connected. Optimising one while ignoring the rest rarely fixes the business.

04

Marketing and sales should speak the same language.

ICP, MQL, SQL, opportunity and revenue cannot mean different things to different teams, or the funnel maths stops being real.

05

Data should improve judgement, not replace it.

Analytics tells me where the system is breaking. Experience and judgement tell me what to investigate next.

06

AI should increase leverage.

I use it for research, audience intelligence, content velocity and campaign optimisation, while strategy and judgement stay human-led.

Artificial intelligence

AI is becoming part of the marketing operating system.

I see AI less as another marketing channel and more as a leverage layer across the function. It changes how much high-quality thinking a team can apply, not what good marketing is. At BlueKaktus this is what it actually looked like in practice.

Audience intelligence

Research, segmentation, ICP refinement and account prioritisation.

Content systems

Research, first drafts and campaign concepts in Claude and ChatGPT, visuals produced in Canva. Four of us built over 15 assets across three verticals that way.

Campaign optimisation

The native AI inside LinkedIn Ads and Google Ads, plus faster analysis, experimentation and targeting.

Marketing operations

Email sequencing and follow-up automation through Instantly, plus workflows and reporting.

The weekly loop

Performance data fed back in every week to build the reporting and decide what to change next. The loop matters more than the tools.

The objective is not to replace marketing judgement. It is to increase the amount of high-quality judgement a marketing team can apply.

The honest caution, which I would say in the room: AI makes it very easy to produce a great deal more of something that was not working. So the measures I would hold it to are cost per qualified opportunity and sales cycle length, not volume, and I would hold a control out from the start.
